The short version is this: Kit is best when audience growth and publishing speed matter most. ## First, the cost model: three different ways to buy a newsletter

I am using the same assumptions throughout: **5,000 subscribers**, multiple free and paid tiers, and up to **50,000 email sends per month**. Each estimate includes the recurring services required to make the system useful, rather than the software license alone. The fixed figures reflect specific plans at this subscriber level: Kit’s **$89** is its Creator plan billed monthly, and Ghost’s **$239** is its Business plan billed monthly, which includes priority support and lands at **$199/mo** if billed annually.

That distinction matters. A platform can advertise a low monthly price while leaving hosting, email delivery, security, setup, or maintenance to the customer. The number on the checkout page is not always the number in the operating budget.

| Platform | Working monthly estimate | What the estimate includes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Kit](https://partners.kit.com/mo5dw8gkbfsr-uw04r) | **$89/mo** | Newsletter platform, sending, audience tools, and reliable managed infrastructure |
| [Ghost](https://ghost.org/) | **$239/mo** | Managed or hosted publishing, email distribution, membership capability, and platform costs |
| [WordPress](https://savvycal.com/joetaylorjr/jt-hoagiestack-discovery-call) | **$149/mo** | Hosting, email distribution, amortized expert setup, licensed plugins, and annual maintenance |

The annual totals are **$1,068 for Kit**, **$2,868 for Ghost**, and **$1,788 for WordPress**. That spread is large enough that price alone may decide the purchase for some writers. The more important cost is attention: how often does the system interrupt the work?

That is the question I use with clients. A newsletter is more than a website. It is a publishing operation. As I have argued elsewhere, [a website is often an operations problem rather than a marketing problem](https://johnsandtaylor.com/articles/your-website-isnt-a-marketing-problem-its-an-operations-problem/).

## Kit: choose it when growth is part of the job

![A newsletter writer sending content into a connected audience network](../../assets/blog/kit-vs-ghost-vs-2.webp)

Kit is the easiest recommendation for the writer who wants to publish quickly and does not want to become the person responsible for infrastructure.

The appeal is straightforward: write, send, segment the audience, and let the platform handle the machinery. Kit’s Creator Network can recommend content across other lists, which gives a growing newsletter something that self-hosted systems generally do not provide: built-in audience discovery. That does not guarantee growth. It does create more opportunities for the right reader to encounter the work.

For a journalist, analyst, or independent writer who is still building an audience, that difference can outweigh several advanced publishing features. Getting 200 relevant new subscribers through recommendations is more useful than owning a technically elegant system that nobody finds.

Kit is also strong when the newsletter has several audience paths. A free weekly edition, a paid research briefing, a welcome sequence, and a digital product can all live inside one audience system. The platform’s tagging and segmentation tools are designed for that kind of work.

The tradeoff is ownership. Kit is not self-hosted. The list, interface, and publishing environment remain dependent on Kit’s service and terms. Exporting subscribers is possible, but recreating the entire experience somewhere else is not a one-click operation.

That may be an acceptable trade. In my experience, reliability is a feature. A writer who spends **$89 per month** and publishes consistently may be better served than one who spends less on software but loses three weekends configuring servers, authentication records, and email delivery.

**My verdict:** Choose Kit if the writer wants speed, dependable delivery, and built-in discovery, and is comfortable paying for a managed service.

Kit is the wrong fit for someone whose primary requirement is complete control over the stack or a highly customized publication experience.

## Ghost: choose it when writing should feel familiar

![A polished publishing workspace representing Ghost’s fast newsletter workflow](../../assets/blog/kit-vs-ghost-vs-3.webp)

Ghost is the closest of these three to the publishing experience many Substack writers already understand. The interface is focused. The relationship between post, website, subscriber, and newsletter is clear. A writer can usually move from idea to draft to publication faster than on a heavily configured WordPress installation.

That speed is not trivial. Every extra screen between the thought and the published post creates friction. Ghost removes much of it.

Ghost is particularly good for the writer who wants a real publication rather than a collection of email blasts. Posts live on a branded website. Subscribers can receive newsletters. Paid memberships can sit alongside free content. The overall model is coherent, which is why I often recommend it to people who say, “I want something like Substack, but I do not want my business tied to Substack.”

At **$239 per month** under this comparison, Ghost is priced on its monthly-billed Business plan, which includes priority support and lands at **$199/mo** with annual billing. Its value is different. Ghost gives the writer a more publication-oriented home, with a clean editorial workflow and an integrated membership model. It also avoids a platform percentage on paid subscriptions, leaving payment processing as the main transaction cost.

The limitation is amplification. Ghost does not give a writer the same built-in recommendation engine that makes Kit attractive for cross-list discovery. A Ghost publication has to earn attention through search, social distribution, referrals, partnerships, and the quality of the work itself.

Self-hosting Ghost is possible, but I would not describe it as a casual weekend project for most journalists. Hosting, updates, backups, email delivery, domain configuration, and security become somebody’s responsibility. Ghost’s managed service is faster; self-hosting offers more control but adds operational work.

**My verdict:** Choose Ghost if the writer wants a clean, Substack-like publishing experience, a branded publication, and the fastest route from setup to sending.

Ghost is the wrong fit for someone who expects the platform itself to provide substantial audience growth, or for someone who wants to self-host without being willing to maintain a server.

## WordPress: choose it when ownership beats convenience

![A modular WordPress newsletter stack with plugins, hosting, security, and maintenance tools](../../assets/blog/kit-vs-ghost-vs-4.webp)

WordPress is the broadest option and the least prescriptive. That is both its strength and its warning label.

A WordPress newsletter can use different hosting companies, email providers, membership plugins, payment systems, analytics tools, and themes. If one vendor becomes too expensive or stops serving the publication well, the site can usually move elsewhere. That portability is valuable. It means the writer owns a stack assembled from replaceable parts rather than a single closed environment.

Licensed plugins can handle memberships, subscriptions, gated content, forms, audience management, and other tasks. WordPress can also support a broader publication: articles, archives, podcasts, events, sponsorship pages, staff bios, and a newsroom workflow.

But WordPress asks for more decisions. Which host? Which email service? Which membership plugin? Who updates the plugins? Who notices when a security patch is urgent? Who restores the site if an update breaks something?

The **$149 per month** estimate assumes those responsibilities are not being ignored. It reflects the managed setup Johns & Taylor runs for clients, covering monthly hosting and email distribution plus expert setup and annual maintenance spread across the year. The maintenance is not decorative. A WordPress installation that is never updated becomes an attractive target for hacking and a poor foundation for a publication.

WordPress also lacks built-in network amplification. It can support excellent search optimization and integrations, but it will not automatically place a new essay in front of another publisher’s audience. The writer must build distribution independently.

As someone who has worked on [content operations and digital systems](https://johnsandtaylor.com/category/content/content-operations/), I see WordPress as the best long-term choice for a writer building something larger than a newsletter. It is less appealing for somebody who simply wants to send one excellent email every Friday.

**My verdict:** Choose WordPress if the writer wants to own the whole stack, expects the publication to expand, and can tolerate, or budget for, maintenance.

WordPress is the wrong fit for anyone who wants the least possible administration.

## The practical decision: match the platform to the writer

I would use these four questions:

1.  **Does audience discovery matter right now?**  
    If yes, start with Kit. Its recommendation network gives growth-minded writers an advantage the other two do not match directly.
    
2.  **Does the writer want to publish without learning a system?**  
    If yes, choose Ghost. Its interface is the most familiar for someone leaving Substack and the fastest to operate out of the box.
    
3.  **Does the publication need to become a broader business?**  
    If yes, consider WordPress. Its ecosystem is harder to manage but easier to extend.
    
4.  **Who will handle the unpleasant work?**  
    Kit handles most of it. Ghost handles much of it when managed. WordPress requires an explicit answer.
    

A writer can assemble a stack from off-the-shelf parts, use a focused managed service, or build nearly everything from scratch. None of those choices is better than another. The mistake is buying the biggest system because it has the most features when what was wanted was somewhere to publish.

For most independent writers, my recommendation is simple: choose **Kit for growth**, **Ghost for writing**, or **WordPress for ownership**. Start with the operating model, not the feature checklist. The best platform is the one that keeps the writer publishing for the next **12–18 months**, not the one that looks most impressive during a Saturday afternoon comparison.
