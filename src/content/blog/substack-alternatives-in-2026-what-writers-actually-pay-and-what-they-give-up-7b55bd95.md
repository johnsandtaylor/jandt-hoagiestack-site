I've been building custom content management tools for newsrooms and media businesses since 1998, and I've watched platforms change underneath the people using them more than once.

So when you pick a newsletter platform, what matters most is **who controls your relationship with readers when the terms change**.

Substack is attractive because it compresses a lot of work into one account: writing, web publishing, email delivery, paid subscriptions, payment processing, and audience discovery. Its [Going Paid page](https://substack.com/going-paid) says writers keep **90% of subscription revenue minus payment-processing fees**.

Substack says writers own their intellectual property, mailing list, and subscriber payments. That's important. But owning the list isn't the same as owning the whole publishing environment around it: the interface, discovery network, sending infrastructure, account access, and payment flow still sit inside Substack.

## What Substack actually costs

For a free newsletter, Substack's direct platform cost is effectively **$0 per month**. For a paid newsletter, the main platform cost is **10% to Substack**, plus Stripe and related processing charges.

Substack's own February 22, 2024 post says **Recommendations plus the app drove 50% of all new subscriptions and 25% of new paid subscriptions**; its October 31, 2024 post says the app had become the primary source of growth, with **3 million subscriptions in the preceding month versus 2 million from Recommendations**. Those figures help explain the fee. See Substack's posts here: [Recommendations network](https://on.substack.com/p/substacks-recommendations-network) and [the app growth post](https://on.substack.com/p/the-substack-app-is-now-the-most).

That 10% pays for the software, the convenience, the network, and the payout system across the platform.

## Why Substack should be a deliberate choice

I advise clients on audience trust, reputation, and operational risk, so for me this is a business-association question more than a speech one. The Guardian reported on February 7, 2026, how Substack makes money from hosting Nazi newsletters ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2026/feb/07/revealed-how-substack-makes-money-from-hosting-nazi-newsletters)). In January 2024, TechCrunch and NBC News reported Substack's hands-off position: it argued that censorship and demonetization don't work, rather than committing to proactively remove that content ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/01/09/substack-nazi-content-policies-controversy/), [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/internet/pressure-builds-newsletter-company-substack-stop-paying-nazi-writers-rcna132593)). Writers left over it; The Washington Post covered Platformer's departure in January 2024 ([The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/01/11/substack-platformer-nazis/)). It kept resurfacing, including a July 2025 push-notification incident and apology reported by Ars Technica ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2025/07/substacks-nazi-problem-wont-go-away-after-push-notification-apology/)).

If you run a publication under your own name, that matters. Because Substack takes a percentage of paid subscription revenue, that same revenue share helps fund payouts across the platform, including to writers many publishers won't want to be in business with. That's the business association any publisher should weigh before they sign up.

## Kit: The best fit if you want managed growth tools

I'd look at Kit first if you want to leave Substack without giving up every growth mechanism.

As noted above, the **$89** figure is the Creator plan at 5,000 subscribers, billed monthly. The reason to pay for that tier is the **Creator Network**: it can put your publication in front of readers on other participating lists through recommendations, which is one of the few real alternatives to relying only on social media, search, and personal outreach.

Kit is also a managed service, so you're not taking on server maintenance, plugin updates, or a self-hosting project. The limitation is straightforward: Kit isn't self-hosted, so your audience and workflow still live inside Kit's infrastructure.

## Ghost: The closest thing to a Substack-style stack

Ghost is the most familiar alternative if you want a publication website, member accounts, email newsletters, and paid subscriptions in one coherent interface.

As noted above, the **$239** figure is Ghost's Business plan billed monthly without the annual discount; billed annually, the same plan is **$199/mo**, and it includes priority support. Ghost prices by member count, so a 5,000-member list lands on Business rather than a lower tier. Its official [pricing page](https://ghost.org/pricing/) lists managed plans, unlimited email sends, member import and export, paid subscriptions on qualifying plans, and **0% platform transaction fees**. Stripe still charges its own processing fees.

Ghost is the fastest out-of-the-box solution of the three. It looks and behaves like a publishing product rather than a general-purpose website system, which lowers the switching cost if you want to keep publishing without a long rebuild. The trade-off is growth: you shouldn't assume Ghost will replace Substack's built-in amplification on its own.

Self-hosting Ghost is possible. I wouldn't call it the easy version.

## WordPress: The most portable, most demanding option

WordPress is the most assembled option: choose the parts, put them together, and accept that somebody has to keep them maintained.

A WordPress newsletter can move between hosts more easily than a tightly integrated hosted platform. Licensed plugins can support memberships, gated content, forms, payment flows, and email distribution. The [WordPress plugin directory](https://wordpress.org/plugins/) lists more than **73,000 free plugins**, which is both an advantage and a warning.

At **$149 per month**, the Hoagiestack estimate includes monthly hosting and email-distribution costs, plus amortized expert setup and annual maintenance, with Stripe fees and the annual domain cost still separate.

WordPress offers the strongest long-term flexibility, but it also means somebody has to stay on top of plugin updates, theme maintenance, hosting, and security, because a neglected WordPress site can become vulnerable fast.

There's also no built-in recommendation network comparable to Substack's. WordPress gives you control; it doesn't automatically give you an audience.

## Fees versus responsibility

The arithmetic changes when your newsletter starts producing real paid revenue.

If you're paying Substack **10%** of subscription revenue, then a publication doing **$900 per month** in paid subscriptions is giving up about **$90** to the platform before Stripe fees. That's the point where the fixed-cost alternatives in this comparison—roughly **$89 to $239 per month**, plus Stripe and domain assumptions where applicable—start to become worth a harder look. It's not a universal break-even point, but it's a better working lens than repeating a larger round-number example.

There's no prize for owning more technology than you can maintain.

## What I'd recommend instead

If you can tolerate the worst conditions—terms changing overnight, an outage you can't route around, a platform whose associations you don't control—Substack can work for you. Plenty of writers are happy there. If you want the closest managed alternative with growth help, start by looking at **Kit**.

Choose **Kit** if you want managed reliability and recommendation-driven growth tools, and you can live without self-hosting.

Choose **Ghost** if you want the most familiar Substack-like publishing experience with more control and no platform share of subscription revenue.

Choose **WordPress** if portability, customization, and long-term ownership matter more than simplicity, and if you can budget for ongoing maintenance.

Before you migrate anything, export your list, reread the current terms, run the 10% against your real revenue, and figure out what you'd do during a 24-hour outage.
